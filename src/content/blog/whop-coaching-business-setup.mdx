## Pick a problem you can help someone work through

A focused offer might help a small merchant improve product photography with their existing phone and lighting. That is clearer than promising to “transform their ecommerce business.” It also gives both sides something concrete to evaluate.

Write down the starting situation, the work the client must do, and the evidence you will review. For the photography example, the client supplies three current listing images, completes a practice shoot, and leaves with a repeatable setup checklist. Better images may support the business, but the coach should not guarantee sales growth from those sessions.

Whop’s [coaching documentation](https://docs.whop.com/supported-business-models/coaches) describes packaging coaching offers and collecting payments. Its [coaching-business guide](https://whop.com/blog/start-a-coaching-business/) offers broader launch context. Treat success stories as individual examples, not typical earnings forecasts.

## Turn the process into a package

Here is an illustrative four-week offer:

| Stage | Coach provides | Client completes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before the first call | Brief and preparation instructions | Uploads current images and describes constraints |
| Week one | Setup review and live demonstration | Builds a repeatable shooting setup |
| Week two | Feedback on a practice set | Reshoots one product |
| Week four | Final review and next-step plan | Delivers a finished image set |

Specify the number and length of sessions, feedback limits, support channel, and access duration. If a client misses a session, the package should explain what happens next.

Use the [package pricing guide](/blog/whop-coaching-packages-pricing) to account for preparation and asynchronous support, not just the time spent on calls.

## Create the product and customer path

Set up the product with its actual price and billing arrangement. Add only the apps needed for delivery: perhaps Calendar Bookings, Files, and a place for questions. A course library is optional, not a prerequisite for selling coaching.

Check that buying the product unlocks the intended material and that a client can schedule without a surprise extra charge. The [booking guide](/blog/whop-calendar-bookings-guide) covers appointment details and time-zone checks.

## Make the sales page specific

Explain who the package is for and who would be better served by a different option. A beginner with no product ready to photograph may need foundational material before a review session is useful.

Show the process, a sample deliverable you created, and any genuine qualifications relevant to the work. If you use a demonstration project, label it as one. Do not present invented client feedback or a staged example as a customer result.

## Learn from a small first cohort

Track whether clients complete the preparation, attend sessions, and produce the agreed work. Ask which instruction was unclear and where they became stuck. Those answers are more actionable than counting how many resources you uploaded.

Review delivery time as well as customer progress. If written feedback takes twice as long as planned, tighten the scope or change the package before adding more clients.
