Whop can connect a service offer to payment, bookings, files, and a client workspace. It works best when you decide what the customer is buying before choosing the apps. A clear deliverable is more useful than a portal filled with empty tabs.

## Choose where Whop belongs in the service

Whop’s [agency services documentation](https://docs.whop.com/supported-business-models/agency-services) describes both payment collection and delivery through private client spaces. You can keep an existing project-management process or use Whop apps for more of the customer experience. That is a workflow decision, not a requirement to move every tool.

Consider a freelance product photographer selling a listing-image package. Payment might happen through Whop while the actual shoot and image editing happen elsewhere. The client space can hold the brief, appointment information, and final files. Whop does not perform the photography or decide whether the work meets the brief.

| Service component | Example promise | What the customer needs to see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inputs | Photograph three supplied products | Shipping instructions and a deadline for the brief |
| Deliverable | Twelve edited images | File format, dimensions, and permitted use |
| Review | One consolidated revision round | How to submit feedback and what counts as a revision |
| Completion | Final files after approval | Delivery location and support period |

These are illustrative package terms. Choose commitments you can fulfill consistently.

## Build one offer before building a catalog

Write the offer in a paragraph: who it is for, what they provide, what you deliver, and when delivery begins. If the schedule starts only after products arrive, say that. “Seven days” means little without a starting event.

Next, separate included work from optional work. Reshooting a product because the client changes its packaging is different from correcting an editing mistake. Price an add-on or quote it separately instead of letting the checkout description imply unlimited revisions.

Whop’s [product setup guide](https://docs.whop.com/manage-your-business/products/create-product) covers product details, pricing, and app access. Use a one-time purchase for a defined project or recurring billing for a continuing service. A monthly charge alone does not define the monthly deliverable; our [consulting retainer guide](/blog/whop-consulting-retainers) explains that distinction.

## Connect payment to the next action

Decide what happens after a successful payment. A photo client may need to complete a brief before booking a call. A consultant may need the booking first. Put that next step near the top of the client experience.

For a negotiated project, review the [invoice workflow](/blog/whop-invoices-guide). For a standard offer, a product checkout link may be enough. Do not make the customer buy the same service again when they reach the calendar.

If you deliver through Whop, grant access only to the apps included in the purchased product. Check with a customer-level account that the buyer can find the brief and files. An administrator seeing everything is not evidence that client permissions are correct.

## Keep a delivery record

Maintain a small record for each project: agreed scope, payment state, inputs received, promised date, files delivered, and approval. That record helps answer a late-delivery question without reconstructing scattered messages.

Start with a manageable pilot. Ask whether clients understood the offer, reached the correct workspace, and knew what to do next. A useful improvement might be clearer shipping instructions rather than another app.

Whop is a reasonable option to evaluate when payment and client access are becoming fragmented. If your current delivery system works, connect it deliberately instead of rebuilding it on launch day.
