Upgrade a Shopify plan when the features or total operating savings justify the added commitment. Revenue alone is not a universal upgrade trigger. If you are comparing payment fees, calculate the saving using only the transactions affected by the change.

This worksheet helps with the fee part of the decision. For the overall plan and feature comparison, start with the [Shopify pricing guide](/blog/shopify-pricing-2026-every-plan-real-cost).

## Gather comparable quotes

Write down the current and proposed subscription prices in the same currency and on the same billing basis. Then list the processing rates and any additional transaction fees for the payment methods your customers actually use.

| Input | Current plan | Proposed plan |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription per comparable period | Your quote | Your quote |
| Eligible payment percentage | Your rate | Your rate |
| Fixed fee per eligible payment | Your rate | Your rate |
| Additional platform fee, if applicable | Your rate | Your rate |
| Other operating savings | Observed baseline | Defensible estimate |

Shopify's [pricing-plan FAQ](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/intro-to-shopify/pricing-plans/FAQ) identifies fee savings as one reason to consider an upgrade. Use your own agreements rather than a universal revenue threshold from an old comparison article.

## The simple break-even formula

If only a percentage fee changes, divide the added subscription cost by the rate reduction written as a decimal. A reduction from 3% to 2.8% is 0.2 percentage points, or 0.002. It is not a 0.2 multiplier.

In a hypothetical comparison, the proposed plan costs $60 more per month and reduces the applicable fee by 0.002. The fee-only break-even is $60 ÷ 0.002 = $30,000 of eligible monthly payment volume.

At $20,000 eligible volume, the modeled saving is $40, so the store is $20 behind on this narrow comparison. At $40,000 eligible volume, the saving is $80, leaving $20 after the added subscription cost. These figures demonstrate the method; they are not Shopify price quotes.

## Account for the actual payment mix

Suppose a store reports $40,000 in sales, but only $24,000 uses the payment method whose fee improves. The saving in the example is $48, not $80. A headline sales figure can therefore make an upgrade look better than it is.

If a fixed charge changes as well, multiply the reduction by the number of eligible payments and add it to the percentage saving. Separate refunds, currency charges, and unaffected payment methods. Our [payment-provider comparison](/blog/shopify-payments-vs-stripe-vs-paypal) explains why processing and additional platform fees should be distinct inputs.

## Value features without inventing savings

A needed staff permission, workflow, or operational capability may justify a higher plan even when fees do not. Write down the actual problem it solves and how you will test the improvement.

For a time-saving feature, estimate the hours genuinely removed from paid work. Ten minutes saved on an occasional task does not become a full employee's salary. For a feature intended to raise conversion, treat the improvement as unproven until the store has evidence.

## Check timing before confirming the change

Shopify's [billing-plan-change documentation](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/your-account/manage-billing/managing-your-bills/billing-plan-changes) describes proration, upgrade timing, and billing-cycle effects. Save the quoted amount due and the next renewal date before you approve a change.

Re-run the [store budget](/tools/shopify-startup-cost-calculator) with the proposed fixed cost and fee assumptions. A subscription that saves money over a modeled year can still create an inconvenient upfront cash payment if the billing cycle changes.

Keep the worksheet with the date and source of each rate. Review it when your payment mix, order value, or operating requirements change. The right upgrade point is the one supported by your store's economics and needs.
