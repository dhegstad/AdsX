Your first Shopify sale and your first usable bank deposit are different events. Payment capture, settlement, the payout schedule, and bank processing each affect when money is available. Before launching, budget for supplier and delivery costs that can fall due before the customer payment reaches your account.

This guide explains the operating sequence and provides a hypothetical planning example. Use the dates shown in your own payment account; the example is not a payout promise.

## What happens between the sale and the bank deposit?

| Stage | What it means for planning | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Payment captured | The payment has moved beyond authorization | Order payment status |
| Settlement | Funds become available for payout | Provider and country rules |
| Payout sent | Provider initiates transfer | Scheduled or issued payout record |
| Bank arrival | Money is available in the destination account | Bank transaction, not the order total |

Shopify's [payout timing documentation](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/shopify-payments/payouts/payout-timing) explains how settlement and payout scheduling differ. Country, payment method, account reviews, reserves, weekends, and bank processing can affect the timeline. Third-party processors follow their own schedules; Shopify's [getting paid guide](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/getting-paid) distinguishes those providers.

For a concrete regional reference, the [United States payout guide](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/shopify-payments/supported-countries/united-states/payouts) currently lists a minimum settlement time of three business days and explains that new merchants can initially have longer settlement times. A minimum is not a guaranteed arrival date.

## Build a cash calendar for your first orders

Suppose you start with $300 available and receive ten orders on Monday. Each order produces $50 in customer receipts, while the supplier and shipping bills total $25 per order. Assume, solely for this illustration, that the payout arrives the following Monday.

| Event | Cash movement | Available cash before payout |
|---|---:|---:|
| Opening reserve | +$300 | $300 |
| Monday: ten supplier orders | −$250 | $50 |
| Wednesday: five additional supplier orders | −$125 | −$75 |
| Following Monday: first payout | Amount depends on fees and adjustments | Arrives after the shortfall |

The business needs at least another $75 to execute that sequence, even though customers have already placed orders. A reserve exactly equal to $75 would leave no space for a replacement, a delayed payout, or an additional bill. Choose the buffer from your actual obligations and uncertainty.

Do not add the $500 Monday sales total to available bank cash before the deposit arrives. Also do not count the supplier purchase twice: it is a cash outflow now, while product cost enters the order economics when the unit is sold.

## Test a slower payout and a faster order day

Run two additional versions of the calendar. In one, move the deposit several days later. In another, double orders while keeping supplier payment due immediately. Growth can increase the amount of cash tied up before a payout.

Include packaging, fulfillment, advertising bills, subscriptions, refunds, and tax amounts that must be set aside. Keep owner withdrawals outside the money needed for obligations. The [10-, 50-, and 100-order budget](/blog/shopify-store-budget-10-50-100-orders) shows contribution at different volumes; this calendar answers the separate question of whether the cash arrives in time.

## Check the right record when a payout is delayed

Read the payout status and account notices. Confirm the bank details, currency, required account information, and any review or hold. Do not repeatedly switch bank accounts as a troubleshooting shortcut; changes themselves can affect processing. Follow the provider's instructions for the specific status shown.

Reconcile the deposit with the payout's transactions and adjustments rather than expecting it to equal a day's gross sales. Fees, refunds, different payment methods, and timing can all create differences.

## Your launch decision

Before accepting orders, identify the earliest bill due, the latest plausible deposit date you are modeling, and the reserve that covers the gap. If the model fails, reduce the initial order commitment, negotiate supplier terms where available, or delay launch until the cash plan works.

Record startup spending in the [Shopify cost calculator](/tools/shopify-startup-cost-calculator), and retain the dated calendar alongside it. Update both after the first real supplier charge and first bank deposit.
