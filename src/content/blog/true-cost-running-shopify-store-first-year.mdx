A useful first-year Shopify budget separates the cost to prepare the store, the cost to keep it running, and the cost to fulfill each sale. There is no dependable universal number for “what a Shopify business costs,” because a digital creator, a stocked product brand, and a made-to-order shop have different requirements.

Use the worksheet below with actual quotes and clearly labeled assumptions. The example figures are fictional, not typical merchant spending or a forecast of your sales.

## Build four budget sections

| Section | Include | Timing question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial setup | Samples, design work, initial photography, configuration | What must be paid before the first order? |
| Fixed operation | Platform, apps, domain, email, ongoing support | What renews even with no sales? |
| Per-order costs | Product, packaging, fulfillment, shipping, payment costs | What is spent each time an order is fulfilled? |
| Growth and change | Acquisition tests, new assets, extra integrations | What is optional and subject to a spending decision? |

[Shopify's billing guide](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/your-account/manage-billing/billing-charges) explains recurring, usage-based, and one-time platform charges. Some external business costs will never appear on the Shopify invoice, so that invoice alone is not a complete store budget.

## Quote the required software stack

Use [Shopify's pricing page](https://www.shopify.com/pricing) for the merchant country and billing term. Record the normal price after any introductory offer. Include only apps that serve a documented requirement, and record their own billing terms.

A domain and business mailbox may renew through other providers. Paid themes or implementation services are typically separate decisions. A free theme and a small initial app stack can be sufficient if they pass the store's requirements.

Use the [plan and fee guide](/blog/shopify-pricing-2026-every-plan-real-cost) to avoid confusing processing, platform transaction fees, and subscription prices.

## Example: a small stocked-product store

Assume the following fictional plan:

| Input | Assumption |
| --- | ---: |
| Samples and setup work | $350 once |
| Initial stock purchase | $800 once |
| Fixed software and services | $75 per month |
| Product revenue per order | $35 |
| Product cost per order | $11 |
| Packaging and fulfillment | $2 |
| Shipping subsidy | $4 |
| Payment cost allowance | $1.35 |
| Expected returns/support allowance | $1.65 |
| Contribution before acquisition and fixed costs | $15 per order |

The initial stock purchase is a cash-flow requirement. The product cost per order is part of the contribution calculation. Do not add both as if they were unrelated expenses for the same units when calculating profit.

At 20 orders in a month, the example creates $300 contribution before acquisition and fixed costs. After $75 fixed costs, $225 remains before acquisition, setup recovery, and other overhead. At five orders, the $75 contribution covers only the modeled fixed software and services.

No sales volume is forecast here. The example shows how to evaluate different outcomes.

## Separate cash from accounting contribution

Cash may leave the business before the associated product sells. Suppliers can require deposits, inventory may arrive in batches, and payouts can have their own timing. Model those dates rather than assuming sales revenue and supplier payments happen together.

For each month, record opening cash, expected receipts, planned payments, and closing cash. Include a no-sales scenario and a delayed-sales scenario. An attractive per-order margin does not prevent a cash shortage before demand arrives.

Choose a cash reserve according to your obligations and uncertainty. Do not use a generic percentage from an article as a substitute for understanding the bills you have committed to pay.

## Account for the operating model

For digital goods, include creation time, revisions, delivery tooling, and support. For print-on-demand, include samples, supplier charges, shipping, and the timing between supplier payment and customer payout. For stocked goods, include storage, defective units, and replenishment.

Shopify's [Digital Products guide](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/digital-service-product/digital-downloads) explains one delivery option for assets. Its [packaging guidance](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/fulfillment/setup/packaging/packages-and-weights) helps physical sellers measure the parcel used for rate calculations.

The [digital-versus-physical comparison](/blog/digital-vs-physical-products-first-shopify-store) provides a separate worked example for choosing a format.

## Set acquisition spending from contribution

In the example above, $15 contribution per order is available before acquisition and fixed costs. Spending $15 to acquire every order leaves nothing from those orders to cover the modeled fixed expenses or produce operating profit.

Choose an acquisition test budget explicitly and record the result. Do not insert an assumed profitable ad campaign into the first-year budget simply because another store reports success.

Organic content, email, and community participation also require work. Account for time even when there is no media bill, while keeping the cash budget distinct from the value of your labor.

## Treat promotions as temporary cash relief

Enter the actual subscription amounts during the promotional period, then the normal amounts afterward. Read the [trial guide](/blog/shopify-free-trial-2026-complete-guide) and preserve the offer presented to your account.

For example, replacing three $40 monthly subscription payments with three $1 payments saves $117 in that hypothetical period. It does not remove the product, shipping, or acquisition expenses, and it does not change the normal post-promotion business model.

## Review the budget after the first orders

Replace assumptions with observed product costs, package costs, support time, returns, and payment charges. Investigate differences before expanding inventory or software spending.

Use the [first 30 days checklist](/blog/first-30-days-shopify-store-checklist) to connect the financial review with actual operations. A useful budget becomes more accurate as the store learns; it should not remain a polished forecast that nobody updates.
