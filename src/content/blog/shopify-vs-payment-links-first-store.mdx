A payment link can be enough when you have a simple offer and already know how customers will find it. A Shopify storefront becomes more useful when buyers need to browse a catalog, compare variants, understand delivery, and return to a recognizable store. Choose the smallest setup that completes your actual selling workflow.

This is a comparison of selling approaches, not a claim that every payment-link service has the same features. Square Payment Links and Shopify's Buy Button illustrate the options; verify the service offered in your country.

## Map the job before selecting the software

| Situation | Start by evaluating | Main test |
|---|---|---|
| One standard workshop deposit | A payment link | Capacity, confirmation, refund handling |
| A small collection sold through social posts | Links or a simple storefront | Variant choice and accurate stock |
| A growing catalog with multiple collections | A storefront | Browsing, search, product details, cart |
| An existing content website with a few products | An embedded purchase option | Compatibility with the site and checkout |
| Custom quotes agreed in conversation | A supported invoice or draft-order workflow | Correct customer, price, and delivery terms |

[Square Payment Links](https://squareup.com/us/en/payment-links) supports shareable checkout links, QR codes, custom fields, and connections with Square's other tools. Its US page advertises no monthly fee for payment links, with transaction charges. That does not make every payment method or associated service free.

Shopify's [Buy Button channel](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/online-sales-channels/buy-button) can add product purchasing to an external website. It is not a recommendation to embed Buy Buttons inside a Shopify storefront. Confirm the current plan, payment eligibility, and setup for your use case.

## A link is only one part of an order

Write down what happens after payment. Who receives the order? How is stock adjusted? How does the customer provide an address or customization? Who sends delivery instructions? What happens if two buyers try to purchase the final unit?

A low-cost checkout can still require manual work. Equally, a complete storefront can add work you do not yet need. For a social seller, our [DM-to-Shopify workflow](/blog/social-media-dm-orders-to-shopify) separates standard product links from individually prepared orders.

## Compare costs at your expected volume

Use the same sales volume, average order value, payment mix, and refund assumptions for both options. Include subscriptions, processing, optional software, and your time handling orders. Do not compare one platform's annual equivalent price with another's monthly cash payment without labeling the difference.

Hypothetically, if option A has no fixed subscription but costs $0.50 more per order, and option B has $40 in fixed monthly costs, the modeled fee break-even is 80 orders. At 20 orders the difference is $10; at 100 orders it is $50. These are illustrative inputs, not current quotes or a complete platform comparison.

If option B also saves a repetitive task, measure that time during a pilot. Include the setup and maintenance time as well. Use the [startup cost calculator](/tools/shopify-startup-cost-calculator) to keep the cash commitment visible.

## Run the same purchase test on both

Create one representative offer and walk through it on mobile. Check product information, payment, confirmation, fulfillment, cancellation, and the customer support route. Add a second item if your real customers will want to build a basket.

Keep the payment-link approach if it handles the offer cleanly and your acquisition plan already exists. Test a storefront when catalog navigation, consistent product information, or operational coordination becomes a real requirement. For a fuller platform comparison, see [Square Online versus Shopify](/blog/shopify-vs-square-online). A new store is useful when it solves the next business problem, not simply because it has more features.
