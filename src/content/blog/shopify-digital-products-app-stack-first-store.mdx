For a first digital-product store, start with the buying and delivery journey: a customer understands the product, pays, receives the correct file, and can get help. Build the app stack around that journey before adding memberships, affiliates, subscriptions, or elaborate email automation.

Shopify can sell digital products, but file delivery needs a suitable app. Shopify’s own [Digital Products documentation](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/digital-service-product/digital-downloads) describes its free app for attaching and delivering files. Do not confuse selling a non-physical product with having an automated file-delivery workflow already configured.

## Map the minimum viable stack

| Customer job | First setup to investigate | Reason to add something more |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand what they are buying | Product page with previews, format, and usage details | A specialized course or interactive demo needs another experience |
| Pay for the product | Store checkout and supported payment setup | A required payment method is unavailable in your market |
| Receive the purchase | A digital delivery app | You need licensing, streaming, memberships, or other unverified capabilities |
| Ask for help | A clear support contact and delivery instructions | Message volume requires a dedicated support workflow |
| Hear about new releases | An optional, clearly described subscription | A measured need for segmentation or automation develops |

The point is not a fixed app count. A PDF checklist business and a video membership have different requirements even though both sell digital products.

## Test one representative product

Choose a product that exercises the real workflow: the expected file format, typical size, number of files, and purchase instructions. Document what the customer should receive and how they recover access if they miss the email.

Create a verification list before selecting a delivery app: supported formats, download behavior, resend options, file updates, customer access, and what happens after a refund. Check each item in the current app documentation rather than assuming that “digital downloads” covers every licensing or access-control need.

Shopify’s [digital products and services guide](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/digital-service-product) is the official starting point for distinguishing these selling models. A downloadable template, a scheduled consultation, and a recurring membership should not share an app recommendation by default.

## Estimate cost at two volumes

Use a planning table with a low-volume month and a busy month. Include the Shopify plan, domain, payment fees, delivery app, and any external service. Record the billing unit for each tool: subscribers, files, downloads, storage, or another measure.

For example, if a fictional delivery tool charges $15 per month plus $0.10 per order above 100 orders, its bill is $15 at 50 orders and $55 at 500 orders. That arithmetic does not describe Shopify’s free app; it demonstrates why an app stack needs a usage model.

See the [app pricing label guide](/blog/shopify-app-free-to-install-vs-free-plan) before putting “free” into the budget. Use current market-specific subscription information from [Shopify’s pricing page](https://www.shopify.com/pricing) rather than assuming a promotion is the ongoing cost.

## Decide whether a full store fits the business

A Shopify store may fit when you want a branded catalog, a broader store experience, or physical and digital products together. A simpler creator platform may fit when your main requirement is a small product catalog and a straightforward delivery flow. Compare the operating work as well as the transaction fee.

Our [Gumroad versus Shopify comparison](/blog/gumroad-vs-shopify-2026) explores that decision. If Shopify fits, follow the [store setup guide](/start-a-shopify-store) and keep this app map beside the launch checklist. You can add complexity once a real customer or operating problem justifies it.
